Reference Design 01 · Discovery, Search & Monetization

Designing a Recommender & Ads Marketplace

Imagine opening a home feed or marketplace search with 10 million eligible items and 150 milliseconds to return 20 results. If you optimize purely for raw clicks, clickbait wins within a week; if you score every item with a deep model, your servers melt. Here is how modern feeds evolve from a week-one baseline into a calibrated multi-stage funnel.

{{recommender}}

1. Narrowing 10 million items down to 500 in 30 milliseconds

Start every interview by stating a zero-ML baseline: serve recent posts from accounts/topics the user follows, backfilled with trending items in their locale. That ships in days, sets your latency floor, and proves your logging pipeline works. It breaks as soon as users want personalized discovery outside their immediate graph—yet running a heavy neural ranker over 10 million items at 0.1 ms each would still take 16 minutes per request. As mapped out in Eugene Yan’s discovery architecture, the universal fix is a funnel: fast candidate generators grab ~500 plausible items in parallel, and an expressive ranker orders only those survivors.

Never bet everything on a single learned retriever. Production systems blend with explicit graph pools (followed creators/topics), lexical or geo-filtered matches, co-engagement (“users who saved X also saved Y”), and a dedicated fresh-item pool—allocating each source’s quota by how much incremental Recall@500 it buys per millisecond. Why multiple pools? Because even a flawless ranker cannot rescue a great post that retrieval never fetched.

Inside that two-tower model sits a classic representation trade-off: . Giving every item ID its own learned vector effortlessly memorizes crowd favorites, until two walls hit at scale. First, a brand-new post has zero clicks, leaving an ID-only tower blind on cold start unless you fuse text or image features—the exact shift LinkedIn made when rebuilding Follows with LLM-adapted semantic embeddings. Second, squeezing hundreds of millions of IDs into a fixed modulo table causes where viral and niche items overwrite the same row; real-time stacks like ByteDance’s Monolith fix this with collisionless lookup tables that automatically expire cold IDs.

To train those towers without scoring all 10 million items on every gradient step, each positive (user, clicked_item) pair reuses the other items in the same minibatch as free negatives. The catch is that viral items appear in almost every batch and get unfairly pushed down unless you apply . Mix those cheap batch negatives with mined hard negatives (near-misses from your current retriever, as detailed in Airbnb’s search retrieval post), and monitor embedding variance across dimensions so you catch (explored deeply in Sumit’s Diary) before recall silently degrades.

2. Turning noisy logs into honest training examples

Before jumping to fancy ranker architectures, walk the interviewer through a concrete feed impression. Suppose at 10:00 AM we show items A (pos 1), B (pos 2), and C (pos 3), while catalog item D was never shown. The user clicks B and hides C. After your attribution window closes (minutes for clicks, days for job applications or purchases so you don’t mislabel slow conversions as negatives!), B is a click positive, A and C are observed click negatives, and C is a positive for a separate hide head. Crucially, unshown D is not an observed dislike—if you sample D for retrieval training, call it an unexposed contrastive negative. And every training feature—like user_clicks_last_7d—must be reconstructed via at 10:00 AM Tuesday, never leaked from Friday’s database snapshot.

3. Ranking, multi-task trade-offs & blending ads fairly

Once retrieval hands over ~500 candidates, one fast ranker is enough until heavy cross-features or user-history Transformers blow past your latency budget—only then do you insert a cheap “light ranker” to trim 500 down to ~60 first (as in LinkedIn’s next-gen Feed pipeline). Unlike retrieval’s separate towers, the heavy ranker sees the user and candidate together, which lets it compute explicit multiplicative interactions via alongside short- and long-term behavior sequences.

In real products, a single click score isn’t enough: you want clicks, saves, and purchases while avoiding hides and quick bounces. Training one shared trunk to predict all of those at once often triggers —where dense click gradients hijack shared weights and hurt sparse conversion or hide predictions (Sumit’s multi-task overview). Gated expert architectures like MMoE or PLE let each task route across shared and private sub-networks, then combine calibrated probabilities into expected user value: Utility = w_click·p(click) + w_save·p(save) − w_hide·p(hide).

What if your feed or search also blends sponsored ads with organic results? First, pass ads through a relevance gate before the auction so a huge bid can never force an irrelevant ad onto the screen. Then score surviving ads in a unified currency alongside organic utility—typically eCPM = bid × pCTR × pCVR − UX_penalty(hide), as detailed in Uber’s Hetero-MMoE ads ranking post. Notice why is a make-or-break interview point here: if your model predicts 2 × pCTR everywhere, offline ROC-AUC and NDCG stay 100% identical, yet you double every ad’s effective bid and break auction pricing!

4. Policy layer, serving budgets & exploration

Raw model scores still aren’t a finished page. Historical logs suffer from position bias—rank 1 gets clicked simply because it’s at the top—which teams mitigate using . After scoring, a lightweight policy layer enforces hard eligibility, creator/category spacing, , and two-sided marketplace congestion caps (recommending the same job or apartment to 50,000 people hurts everyone’s conversion rate). Reserve a small 2–5% exploration budget—using contextual bandits like Thompson sampling, as in Spotify’s homepage bandits and Eugene Yan’s bandit guide—to gather unbiased signal on cold items.

To hit a 150 ms serving budget (say: 35 ms parallel retrieval, 25 ms batched feature-store lookup, 55 ms GPU/CPU scoring, 10 ms policy/auction, 25 ms p99 headroom), precompute static item embeddings offline and stream fast session counters into memory (real-time ML pattern). Suppress already-seen posts cheaply with a per-user , deploy matched query-tower and ANN index versions together, and fall back to cached eligible trending items if the ranker times out.

How interviewers poke at this design

“Brand-new items almost never get shown—where is the bug?” Trace the funnel top-to-bottom: (1) did retrieval drop it because ID embeddings are zero at cold start and there’s no fresh-item candidate pool? (2) did the ranker starve it because historical count features (total_clicks = 0) dominate? or (3) is there zero bandit exploration budget?

“Offline NDCG jumped +2%, so why did 30-day retention drop in A/B?” Check four culprits first: a p99 latency spike triggering degraded fallbacks, train–serve feature skew on a real-time counter, optimizing short-term clickbait (click up, hide/bounce also up), or offline evaluation rewarding the old model’s exposure bias.

Reference Design 02 · Grounded Search & Enterprise QA

Designing Permission-Aware Enterprise RAG

Answering questions over internal filings, Slack threads, and spreadsheets sounds easy in a laptop demo—until a chunk loses which quarter a revenue number came from, a revoked document leaks through a stale cache, or an LLM confidently invents a citation. Good enterprise RAG is mostly an information-retrieval and access-control system with a careful generator at the end.

{{rag}}

1. Why naive chunking breaks—and how to ingest cleanly

Suppose you slice a 100-page quarterly filing into flat 400-token windows. Chunk #47 ends up reading, “Operating revenue grew 3% over the prior period.” Which company? Which quarter? Both facts lived 12 pages earlier in the section header, so neither keyword search nor embeddings can match that chunk to “What was Acme’s Q3 2025 revenue growth?” Instead, split along document structure (headings, paragraphs, intact table rows with column headers and units repeated), keep parent-section pointers so small matched chunks can expand cleanly, and apply Anthropic’s Contextual Retrieval pattern: prepend a short document/section summary (cheaply generated with prompt caching) to every chunk before indexing.

Real workspaces also change every minute: pages get edited, deleted, or unshared. Following Notion’s two-year vector search evolution, separate ingestion into two paths: an offline Spark batch pipeline for bulk backfills or embedding-model upgrades (tagged by chunker + model version), and a sub-minute Kafka streaming pipeline that hashes changed blocks and writes immediate so revoked content disappears right away.

2. Hybrid retrieval, permission boundaries & reranking

Pure vector search is great at synonyms (“layoffs” ↔ “workforce reduction”) and terrible at exact tickers, part numbers, error codes, or person names (ERR_409_CONFLICT, BRK.B). Pair , dynamically weighting exact vs. semantic match as Instacart does in hybrid search. Because unbounded BM25 scores (14.2) and cosine similarities (0.81) live on incompatible scales, merge the two lists parameter-free using .

Permissions must be enforced outside the LLM—never stuff unauthorized text into a prompt and ask the model to “ignore secret passages.” Push tenant and user-group ACL filters directly into the retrieval query (oversampling when filters are tight so top-50 doesn’t come back empty), AND re-check authoritative read permissions on the winning document IDs right before building the prompt, as emphasized in Dropbox’s Dash architecture. Then pass the top ~50 candidates through a down to the best 5–10 deduplicated spans with explicit citation IDs ([doc_14#p3]), reserving plenty of token headroom for the answer.

3. When to retrieve, when to write SQL & how not to leak via cache

Keep your default path deterministic (retrieve → rerank → generate): it gives predictable sub-2-second time-to-first-token and easy debugging. As explored in Sumit’s Adaptive RAG series, add a lightweight router up front for two cases that single-shot prose RAG handles poorly: (1) multi-hop questions (“Compare Acme’s Q3 margin to its main competitor’s”), where a bounded 2–3 step search loop refines follow-up queries under a strict token/latency cap, and (2) arithmetic or aggregations over structured tables, which should route to a read-only SQL/dataframe tool rather than trusting an LLM to sum 40 chunks in its head.

Treat caching and fallbacks as security boundaries. Sharing static system-prompt is a classic interview trap: prefix KV caching is safe, whereas a fuzzy semantic answer cache can easily hand User B a cached answer built from User A’s private memo—or serve Q2’s revenue for a Q3 query because the two questions have 0.97 cosine similarity! Likewise, if your internal GPU cluster times out and falls back to an external commercial API, strip any chunks not cleared for external egress and verify that every returned citation belongs to the exact subset sent.

How interviewers poke at this design

“Answers are wrong 20% of the time—is retrieval or generation to blame?” Run a gold-context eval (as recommended in Applied LLMs): feed the generator the human-verified right passages directly. If accuracy jumps to 96%, your chunking/retrieval/reranker is dropping evidence; if it stays poor, fix prompt instructions, context ordering, or citation-entailment checks.

“The model cites [doc_4], which exists, yet the number in the sentence is still made up!” Checking that doc_4 was in the prompt only proves citation membership, not faithfulness. Split the answer into atomic factual claims and run a lightweight NLI / rubric judge checking whether doc_4’s exact span entails the claim (including matching dates and units), tracking abstention on unanswerable queries separately from false refusals.

Reference Design 03 · Tool Use, State Machines & Evals

Designing an Agent for Enterprise Knowledge Work

When a user asks an AI coworker to pull Q3 variances across Snowflake, Google Sheets, and email—and draft updates back into a tracker—success is no longer a nice paragraph; it is a verified deliverable with zero unauthorized side effects. The secret is keeping the runtime boring and deterministic everywhere the model doesn’t strictly need autonomy.

1. Workflows first, durable state, and why multi-agent writes clash

The overarching lesson from Anthropic’s Building Effective Agents and OpenAI’s Practical Guide to Agents is to resist open-ended autonomy until you need it: if a weekly report always runs the same four queries, code a fixed workflow DAG! Use a dynamic tool-calling loop (surveyed in Lilian Weng’s agent primer) only when tool choice genuinely depends on what the previous step uncovered—and cap every loop by step count, wall-clock seconds, and dollar spend, halting early if the model repeats identical failing calls.

What about spinning up a swarm of subagents? Fanning out parallel workers works great for read-only research—say, searching five independent company filings at once and returning short summaries. It breaks badly when parallel subagents write to shared artifacts simultaneously: as argued in Cognition’s Don’t Build Multi-Agents, two agents make silent, conflicting assumptions that corrupt shared state. Enforce a single-writer coordinator backed by an external durable event log (separate from the rolling prompt, as in Anthropic’s Managed Agents) so a crashed worker container can restart mid-task without losing progress.

2. Context engineering & pruning 100 tools down to 5

Handing an LLM 80 overlapping APIs or a 1,200-column database schema tanks tool-selection accuracy and swamps the context window. Follow the pattern from Uber’s QueryGPT: route the request to a domain workspace first, then prune down to only the relevant tables, columns, and narrow, well-documented tools (Anthropic’s tool-design guide). Instead of dumping a 50,000-row CSV into chat history, use (context engineering): let tools return schemas, row counts, and filtered aggregates while keeping system/tool definitions frozen at the top of the prompt to maximize KV prefix-cache hits.

At the boundary between model and code, use so tool arguments always match your JSON schema—then still validate permissions, ranges, and business rules server-side! Separate read tools from consequential write tools: untrusted external text (like an incoming vendor email) can never authorize a write; stage mutations as a concrete diff preview for human confirmation, bind approval to that exact payload, and attach idempotency keys (a core pattern in Stripe’s agent-assisted risk workflows).

3. Grading outcomes & safely upgrading the backing model

How do you prove model-v2 beats model-v1 (or that routing easy steps to a smaller model is safe) before shipping? Walk through this 5-step harness, grounded in Anthropic’s agent evals guide and Eugene Yan’s LLM-evaluator playbook:

  1. Seed resettable test worlds: Snapshot realistic sandboxes (docs, sheets, emails) containing distractors, conflicting dates, and permission-denied traps—plus a regression bank of past production failures.
  2. Grade final state, not one rigid tool path: Use deterministic checks for final spreadsheet formulas, database rows, and zero forbidden calls. Where semantic judgment is needed, align narrow pass/fail LLM judges against human labels (Shankar et al.) and blind/swap order to avoid verbosity and position bias (Zheng et al.).
  3. Run fresh end-to-end trajectories: Let both incumbent and candidate models drive their own tool choices in isolated sandboxes—replaying the old model’s transcript hides how the new model recovers from its own steps.
  4. Measure and cost per solved task: A cheaper model that loops 4× longer can cost more and fail intermittently; keep ACL/safety violations as hard zero-tolerance gates.
  5. Canary & A/B with instant rollback: Ramp behind a model router while tracking completion rate, user manual edits/retries, rating participation, and p95 latency.

Reference Design 04 · Sandboxed Execution & Verification

Designing a Repository-Scale Coding Agent

A coding agent turns a bug report or feature request on a 20,000-file repository into a tested, reviewable pull request. As OpenAI’s Harness Engineering and Ramp’s Inspect showed, model intelligence is only half the battle: whether the agent ships clean code or broken slop comes down to repo search, edit tooling, and a closed-loop sandbox harness.

The 6-step closed loop from issue to verified PR

  1. Boot a hermetic, pre-warmed baseline. Spin up an isolated container/VM pinned to the exact git commit with compilers, databases, and test runners already warm (the architecture behind Ramp’s background agent). Run the target test suite before editing anything to reproduce the bug and record pre-existing red tests.
  2. Navigate code via exact search + incremental indexing. Start with fast deterministic tools—stack traces, filenames, ripgrep, and symbol/call-graph jumps—reading whole functions rather than stuffing 50 files into the prompt. For conceptual queries across huge repos, Cursor’s codebase indexer pairs with so switching branches or sharing a teammate’s index updates in milliseconds.
  3. Apply minimal, verifiable edits. Have the agent state a concrete hypothesis, apply a surgical search-and-replace block (or use as in Cursor’s Instant Apply when rewriting larger files), inspect git diff, and checkpoint progress to disk (Anthropic’s long-running harness pattern) so long sessions survive context compaction.
  4. Enforce strict sandbox isolation. Run builds and tests in unprivileged, CPU/RAM-capped VMs with allowlisted network egress and zero deploy credentials—treating repo issues, PR comments, and dependency READMEs as untrusted data that cannot exfiltrate secrets.
  5. Verify independently (and guard the tests!). Re-run the reproduction test, neighbor unit/integration tests, typecheckers, linters, and (for UI diffs) headless browser screenshots. In offline benchmarks and CI gates, run hidden acceptance tests and flag test-file deletions—otherwise an over-eager agent may “fix” a failing test by deleting assert!
  6. Deliver a human-reviewable artifact. Emit a minimal PR diff, root-cause explanation, and the exact commands passed/failed (preserving partial branches and logs on timeout).

How interviewers poke at this design

“When does plain grep beat vector embeddings on code—and vice versa?” Exact ripgrep/LSP wins 80% of the time when you have an error string, symbol name (calculateMargin), or stack trace—embeddings blur exact identifier spelling! Semantic search earns its keep on cold entry points like “Where do we retry failed webhook deliveries?” when you don’t know what functions are named yet.

“What if 3% of the repo’s integration tests are flaky?” Quarantine known flaky tests with historical pass-rate metadata, retry isolated failures once on the clean baseline commit to separate pre-existing flake from patch-induced breakage, and never let the agent burn 15 edit loops chasing a random network timeout.

Reference Design 05 · Cold-Start Supervision & Active Learning

Bootstrapping an ML System from Scarce Labels

One of the most common curveballs in ML system design is: “Classify 10 million transactions, merchants, or policy violations at 20 ms latency—except you only have 150 labeled examples and domain experts cost $150/hour.” Standard supervised training stalls immediately; your real job is designing a self-improving data flywheel.

1. Spend your first human hours on the exam, not the textbook

The single biggest mistake candidates make is throwing all 150 expert labels into model.fit() and having nothing trustworthy left to measure progress! Spend your initial SME budget locking down a clean, written labeling rubric (resolving edge cases via double-annotation agreement) and holding out a gold test + calibration set split by time or entity. Every clever trick that follows—rules, zero-shot prompts, teacher models—gets graded strictly against that untouched human benchmark, never against noisy synthetic labels.

With your benchmark locked, ship a Day-1 baseline in hours rather than months: embed inputs with a strong pretrained encoder (CLIP for images+text, a dense bi-encoder for documents) and fit a simple kNN or L2-regularized logistic probe on your tiny seed set—or run a prompted frontier LLM over a small sample to map failure modes, much like Ramp bootstrapped merchant industry classification over messy financial descriptors.

2. Scaling noisy supervision & distilling a fast student

You can’t call a giant frontier LLM synchronously at 5,000 QPS on a 20 ms SLA, and you can’t wait six months for humans to label 100,000 rows. Bridge the gap by generating cheap, large-scale silver labels offline using two complementary engines: LLM-as-Teacher batch labeling (with strict JSON schemas, prompt caching, and multi-prompt agreement filtering) and (domain regexes, allowlists, and cross-table heuristics combined via Snorkel’s label model so three overlapping rules don’t get triple-counted).

Next, train a compact production student—a LightGBM tabular model or a small DeBERTa/8B encoder—via , blending teacher probabilities on unlabeled rows with higher-weighted cross-entropy on your clean human seed set. Because reweighted or synthetic training shifts class priors, finish with Platt or isotonic calibration on your held-out human calibration slice before picking a business cost threshold.

3. Closing the human-in-the-loop flywheel

Once v1 is live (shadowed first, then ramped), turn weekly SME review hours into compounding gains—similar to Stripe’s agent-assisted fraud loop. Instead of labeling random traffic (which wastes 99.9% of human time when positives are 0.1% rare!), route weekly review queues using —always reserving a small 5–10% uniform random slice so you still catch brand-new attack patterns where the student model is confidently wrong and monitor real-traffic precision drift.

How interviewers poke at this design

“Positives are 1-in-5,000 (0.02% abuse). Even labeling 2,000 random rows for your gold test set yields zero positives!” Use stratified / importance sampling: build high-recall candidate buckets (high weak-rule/embedding score, medium score, and uniform background), label within each stratum, and weight each stratum back by its true population share (P(stratum)) so your estimated precision and recall stay mathematically unbiased for real traffic.
