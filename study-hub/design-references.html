Reference Design 01 · Discovery, Feature Stores & Monetization

Designing a Recommender & Ads Marketplace

Imagine opening a home feed or marketplace search with 10 million eligible items and 150 milliseconds to return 20 results. Optimize purely for clicks and clickbait wins within a week; score every item with a deep network and your servers melt; rewrite offline SQL features in online Java and silent bugs sink your A/B test. Here is how Staff-level designs navigate those trade-offs from a week-one baseline to production scale.

{{recommender}}

1. Candidate retrieval & the ID vs. content embedding dilemma

Start every interview with a zero-ML baseline: recent posts from followed accounts/topics backfilled with locale-trending items. That ships in days, proves out logging, and sets your latency floor. Once users need personalized discovery beyond their explicit graph, scoring 10 million items at 0.1 ms each would take 16 minutes—so, following Eugene Yan’s discovery funnel, you retrieve ~500 candidates in parallel using fast sources (, follow-graph lookups, lexical/geo filters, co-engagement graphs, and a dedicated fresh-item pool) before running a heavy ranker. Allocate each source’s candidate quota by marginal Recall@500 per millisecond: a brilliant ranker can never rescue an item retrieval missed.

Inside the item tower, interviewers love probing when and why to use . Think in terms of catalog churn and interaction density:

  • When ID embeddings shine: Low-churn, dense-engagement catalogs (Netflix shows, Spotify tracks, evergreen e-commerce SKUs, top creators). Once item_8492 has 50,000 interactions, a dedicated 64-d lookup vector captures collaborative nuances—humor, pacing, subtle quality—that no text or image encoder can infer from metadata alone.
  • When content/multimodal embeddings shine: High-churn or long-tail feeds (breaking news with a 6-hour lifespan, fresh job listings, classifieds, new short-form videos) where 70% of impressions hit items under 24 hours old. Pure ID tables initialize cold items to zeros/noise, starving them of retrieval recall.
  • How combining both goes wrong (and how to fix it): If you naively sum or concatenate ID_vec + Content_vec, historical logs are dominated by popular warm items—so the network lazily memorizes ID_vec and starves the content weights of gradient! Prevent that with : only mint dedicated ID rows after K interactions, randomly mask item_id → <UNK> on 15–30% of training steps so content features must stay predictive (as in LinkedIn’s LLM-based Follows), and avoid modulo using expiring collisionless tables like ByteDance’s Monolith.

2. What loss should train your retrieval embeddings?

Why don’t modern two-tower systems train with classic pairwise (“double”) contrastive loss or triplet margin loss max(0, d(u, v+) − d(u, v−) + m)? Because once 90% of random negatives sit safely past margin m, 90% of your GPU batch produces exact zero gradient, stalling learning unless you maintain brittle online hard-negative miners. Instead, industrial retrievers use multi-class in-batch softmax (InfoNCE): in a batch of B = 2,048 positive pairs, computing U @ V.T contrasts every user against 2,047 negatives at once, automatically putting the steepest softmax gradient on whichever distractors currently fool the model most!

Three practical subtleties separate senior from staff answers here. First, naive in-batch softmax punishes popular items unfairly because hits appear as negatives in almost every batch—fix this with plus 2–4 mined hard negatives per row (items ranked top-50 by your current retriever that weren’t clicked, as in Airbnb’s search retrieval—filtering out other items the user also liked!). Second, if users routinely engage with several batch items simultaneously, switch softmax to independent pairwise sigmoid loss (SigLIP-style BCE) so valid co-positives don’t fight each other. Third, choose deliberately between cosine vs. raw dot product: L2-normalized cosine + temperature τ strips vector length so popularity can’t hijack similarity (great for search and tail fairness), whereas unnormalized dot product lets ‖v‖ naturally encode a global item-quality prior. Always monitor singular-value spread to catch (Sumit’s Diary).

3. Feature stores: zero online–offline skew & streaming vs. batch tiers

Nothing quietly ruins more ML launches than online–offline skew: a data scientist writes a Spark SQL window feature on historical Hive tables, a backend engineer rewrites “the same” counter in Go + Redis for serving, and subtle differences in null defaults, timezones, or window edges tank online conversion. As documented in Airbnb’s Chronon architecture, there are only two honest ways to eliminate skew:

  1. Log-and-Wait (Request-Time Feature Logging): When the online ranker fetches features from Redis at 10:02:14 AM to score 500 items, write that exact feature vector straight to Kafka alongside request_id. Offline training joins those logged snapshots with matured labels (respecting attribution windows: minutes for clicks, 7 days for job applications or purchases!). Pros: 100% immune to time-travel leakage and code skew. Cons: zero ability to backfill—if you invent a new feature today, you must deploy it and wait weeks to accumulate training logs!
  2. Declarative Write-Once Feature Platform (): Define each transformation once in Python/SQL; the platform compiles that single spec into both Spark (instant 1-year backfills for new feature experiments!) and stateful Flink streaming jobs writing into Redis/DynamoDB—continuously running a 1% parity audit comparing live logged values against offline backfills.

Nor should every feature be streamed in real time! Continuous Flink jobs backed by SSD RocksDB state and high-write Redis clusters cost 3–5× more to operate than nightly Spark jobs (Eugene Yan’s real-time ML guide). Match each feature to the cheapest freshness tier that captures its signal, and use (e.g., summing a 29-day nightly batch counter with today’s live Flink counter) instead of keeping 30 days of raw events in streaming RAM:

4. Heavy ranking, multi-task utility & ads auction calibration

With ~500 candidates hydrated (pruned through a fast GBDT/MLP light ranker first only if sequence math blows your SLA, as in LinkedIn’s Feed), the heavy ranker jointly scores user, context, and item. Combine explicit with a short live-action Transformer (Pinterest’s TransAct, Airbnb’s guest journey) whose user-sequence representation is computed once per request and broadcast across all 500 items—or evaluate whether (Netflix GenRec, Meta HSTU) justify their GPU serving footprint.

Because feeds balance competing behaviors—clicks, long dwell, saves, purchases, and hides—use pointwise cross-entropy heads routed through (Sumit’s multi-task deep dive) so dense click gradients don’t corrupt sparse conversion or hide heads. To blend sponsored ads alongside organic items (Uber’s Hetero-MMoE ads stack), filter ads through a relevance floor before bidding, then merge in a common expected-value currency: AdScore = bid × pCTR × pCVR − UX_penalty(pHide). Emphasize why is mandatory here: uncalibrated logits can still win on NDCG while mispricing millions in ad spend!

Finally, debias historical rank #1 visibility with , diversify the final 20-item slate with and marketplace supply/congestion caps, suppress repeats via a per-user , and reserve 2–5% contextual-bandit traffic (Spotify homepage bandits, Eugene Yan’s bandit guide) so cold items earn unbiased impressions.

How interviewers poke at this design

“Brand-new items almost never get shown—where is the bug?” Check four places in funnel order: (1) ID-only item tower with zero ID-dropout so cold items get useless vectors and lack a dedicated fresh-item retrieval pool; (2) ranker dominated by cumulative historical counters (total_7d_clicks = 0) instead of smoothed rates or content features; (3) no bandit exploration budget; or (4) stale hourly index lag.

“Offline NDCG jumped +2%, so why did 30-day retention drop in A/B?” Suspect online–offline feature skew on a streaming counter first, then p99 latency timeouts triggering unpersonalized fallbacks, clickbait objective weights (click up while hide/bounce also rose), or offline replay rewarding the old ranker’s position bias.

Reference Design 02 · Grounded Search, Reranking & Agentic Navigation

Designing Permission-Aware Enterprise RAG

Answering questions over internal filings, Slack threads, and spreadsheets looks effortless in a prototype—until a 300-token chunk loses which company or quarter a revenue figure came from, a revoked memo leaks via cache, or you have to decide whether an agent should iteratively browse surrounding pages. Great enterprise RAG is an information-retrieval and security architecture first, and an LLM prompt last.

{{rag}}

1. Three ways to solve “lost chunk context” & dual-path ingestion

Slice a 100-page SEC filing into blind 400-token chunks and chunk #47 reads: “Operating revenue grew 3% over the prior period.” The company name and quarter sat 10 pages earlier, so neither BM25 nor dense search matches it to “What was Acme’s Q3 2025 growth?” Staff designs weigh three distinct remedies—plus how to handle tables and live edits:

Context-preservation patternHow it worksIngest cost vs. query latency trade-off
Contextual Retrieval
(Anthropic pattern)		An LLM prepends a 50-token header (“Acme Q3 2025 10-Q, MD&A…”) to each chunk’s text before both BM25 and vector indexing.Higher one-time ingest LLM cost (slashed ~80% via prompt caching over the full doc); zero runtime latency hit and boosts BM25 too!

(Jina pattern)		Run the entire 8k-token doc through a long-context bi-encoder first so token states attend globally, then mean-pool chunk spans afterward.Cheap, fast ingest with zero LLM summary hallucinations; requires a long-context encoder and does not enrich lexical BM25.

Never shred financial or product tables mid-row: serialize column headers, units, entity, and period onto every row chunk, and route arithmetic/aggregations to a read-only SQL/dataframe tool rather than asking an LLM to add 40 chunks in its head. For freshness at scale, follow Notion’s dual-path vector architecture: Spark batch jobs handle bulk backfills and encoder version upgrades, while a sub-minute Kafka stream re-embeds edited blocks by content hash and writes immediate .

2. Embedding training, RRF vs. cross-encoders & permission gates

If off-the-shelf embeddings miss domain jargon, how do you fine-tune retrieval encoders? Unlike symmetric sentence similarity, QA retrieval is asymmetric (a 9-word question matching a 300-word passage), so prepend role prefixes ("query: " vs. "passage: ") and train with L2-normalized cosine InfoNCE (so verbose chunks can’t win via huge norms) over in-batch negatives + mined BM25 hard negatives. Better yet, distill soft margin scores (KL divergence) from an offline cross-encoder teacher so unlabeled “hard negatives” that actually answer the query aren’t falsely pushed away!

At query time, pair (Instacart’s hybrid retrieval), enforcing tenant/ACL filters inside the index query and re-verifying authoritative permissions before prompt assembly (Dropbox Dash). Then interviewers love asking: when do you use heuristic rank fusion vs. a neural cross-encoder? Use both at different funnel depths—never confuse their jobs:

  • is a 0.1 ms CPU list-merger: It combines 200 BM25 hits and 200 vector hits down to ~60 candidates with zero score-calibration fragility. Limitation: RRF never reads a single word of text—if a keyword-stuffed 2023 doc ranks #3 in both lists for a 2025 query, RRF happily promotes it to #1! Use RRF alone only on ultra-cheap, GPU-free <50 ms typeahead/search endpoints.
  • A (100–400M params) is your 30 ms GPU precision filter: Running joint token-to-token attention across those 60 RRF survivors catches negations (“excludes New York”), exact dates, and entity bindings before you pack the top 6–10 deduplicated chunks into the LLM prompt. (Reserve heavy 500 ms LLM listwise rerankers for offline teacher labeling, not live serving.)

3. Should we allow re-querying? Routing, loops & safe caching

Separate two very different kinds of “re-querying” when discussing latency and quality (Sumit’s Adaptive RAG):

  • Pre-retrieval query rewriting / decomposition (~150 ms, almost always worth it): In multi-turn chat, raw user input like “how did that compare to Q2?” fails retrieval completely. A tiny fast model rewrites coreferences into standalone queries, extracts structured metadata filters (ticker="ACME", year=2025), or splits a comparison into 2 parallel searches in one shot.
  • Closed-loop agentic re-querying (+1.5–5s, gate strictly!): Letting the model read retrieved chunks, judge them insufficient, and fire new searches rescues hard multi-hop investigations—but doubles/triples TTFT and risks rabbit-hole drift. Gate iterative loops behind a router or trigger a second hop only when the cross-encoder’s max relevance score falls below a calibrated confidence threshold, hard-capping at 2–3 hops before clean abstention.

Finally, avoid security footguns in caching and fallbacks: static system-prompt is night and day—prefix KV caches are lossless and safe, whereas fuzzy semantic answer caches risk cross-user ACL leaks and serving Q2 numbers for Q3 queries. On internal-cluster timeouts, strip non-external-safe docs before calling an external fallback API and verify every citation ID against the exact subset sent.

How interviewers poke at this design

“Answers are wrong 20% of the time—is retrieval or generation broken?” Run a gold-context test (Applied LLMs) feeding human-verified passages directly to the generator: if accuracy jumps to 95%+, fix chunking/hybrid recall/reranking; if it stays low, fix prompt framing, lost-in-the-middle packing, or table-to-SQL routing.

“Why not just dump the whole 500k-token folder into a long-context LLM and skip RAG?” Do the math aloud: 500k prefill tokens at 10 QPS burns ~$5–$15/sec in GPU compute, pushes TTFT to 10–20 seconds, still degrades on multi-needle reasoning (“context rot”), and doesn’t scale to 50M enterprise docs!

Reference Design 03 · Tool Orchestration, CodeAct & Evals

Designing an Agent for Enterprise Knowledge Work

When an AI coworker pulls Q3 variances across Snowflake, Google Sheets, and email—and stages updates back into a tracker—success is a verified final state with zero unauthorized mutations. Staff architectures stay deterministic everywhere possible, isolate writes behind single-threaded state logs, and prevent tool outputs from rotting the context window.

1. Execution topology (Service RPC vs. Sandbox VM) & interface (MCP vs. CLI)

Start with the golden rule from Anthropic’s Building Effective Agents and OpenAI’s Agent Guide: use a deterministic code DAG whenever steps are fixed, and switch to an agent loop (Lilian Weng) only when step 2 genuinely depends on what step 1 uncovered. Once you need an agent, Staff interviewers increasingly probe two orthogonal architecture choices that people often conflate—where the agent executes versus how tools are exposed:

(“Give the Agent a Computer”: Bash + )		Each session gets an isolated microVM/container with bash, files, Python, and CLIs (jq, duckdb, internal CLIs), decoupled via .Wins: zero schema bloat (runs cli --help on demand), OS pipes (cmd | jq | head) compose in 1 turn (Ronacher), and disk acts as infinite scratchpad memory (Manus).

What about spinning up multi-agent swarms? Parallel subagents shine on read-only fan-out (summarizing five independent 10-Ks simultaneously into clean briefs), whereas parallel writers clash over unspoken assumptions, as shown in Cognition’s Don’t Build Multi-Agents. Keep a single-writer orchestrator backed by an external durable event log (Anthropic’s Managed Agents) with so stateless workers can crash and resume without double-firing completed writes.

2. Tool pruning, memory tiers & write-safety boundaries

Exposing 100 overlapping tools or 1,200 SQL columns degrades tool selection. Route to a domain workspace first and prune down to the relevant tables, columns, and narrow tools (Uber’s QueryGPT, Anthropic’s tool guide). Keep prompts lean via (context engineering), keeping static instructions/schemas at the prompt prefix for KV-cache reuse. Separate three memory tiers cleanly so agents don’t poison themselves over weeks: (1) ephemeral working scratchpad, (2) durable task event log, and (3) user/project memory stored as auditable, timestamped key-value facts with user edit/delete controls—never blindly auto-saving unverified model guesses into a permanent vector DB!

Use for syntax-safe tool calls while validating ranges, ACLs, and business invariants server-side. Isolate untrusted retrieved content from write authority: high-stakes writes stage a diff preview for human sign-off bound to that exact payload and carry idempotency keys (Stripe’s agent-assisted risk workflows).

3. Grading outcomes & safely upgrading the backing model

To verify whether a newer or smaller model improves quality/cost without silent regressions (Anthropic agent evals, Eugene Yan’s evaluator guide):

  1. Seed resettable test worlds: Snapshot realistic sandboxes with distractors, stale docs, and permission traps, plus past production failures.
  2. Grade final state, not one rigid trajectory: Check final database/sheet state and zero forbidden calls deterministically; calibrate narrow binary LLM judges against human labels (Shankar et al.) while blinding/swapping order (Zheng et al.).
  3. Run fresh trajectories end-to-end: Let candidate and incumbent models choose their own actions in isolated sandboxes rather than replaying stale transcripts.
  4. Compare & cost per solved task: Track multi-run reliability alongside total retries/tokens, with zero-tolerance gates on ACL/safety violations.
  5. Canary & A/B behind a router: Ramp gradually while monitoring completion rate, manual user edits/retries, rating participation, and p95 latency.

Reference Design 04 · Repo Graphs, Edit Formats & VM Harnesses

Designing a Repository-Scale Coding Agent

Turning a bug report on a 30,000-file monorepo into a clean pull request tests harness design more than prompt wording. As OpenAI’s Harness Engineering and Ramp’s Inspect demonstrated, Staff-level coding agents live or die on sub-second Copy-on-Write sandboxes, structural repo navigation, reliable edit formats, and tamper-proof verification.

The 6-step closed loop—and the key trade-offs inside each step

  1. Boot instant Copy-on-Write sandboxes. Waiting 6 minutes for docker build && npm install on every task kills interactive UX and parallel eval throughput. Snapshot pre-warmed microVM disk/memory images (Firecracker / OverlayFS, as in Ramp’s Inspect) so isolated checkouts fork in <500 ms with compilers, DBs, and test runners hot; run target tests immediately to confirm baseline state.
  2. Combine lexical search, call-graph RepoMaps & incremental vectors. Don’t rely on vector search alone—chunk embeddings blur exact symbol names and miss cross-file blast radius! Pair exact ripgrep/LSP jump-to-definition with a structural repository map (Aider’s tree-sitter + PageRank RepoMap ranks which imported symbols/files matter most to active files in ~1k tokens), backed by Cursor-style indexing using and for broad conceptual discovery.
  3. Pick a reliable code-edit format (and validate syntax immediately). How the model emits edits dramatically changes patch failure rates (Aider’s edit-format benchmarks): raw line-numbered diffs fail constantly because LLMs miscount line numbers; whole-file rewrites are simple on tiny files but slow on 800-line files unless paired with (Cursor Instant Apply); anchored SEARCH / REPLACE or line-free unified diffs + immediate tree-sitter parse checks bounce malformed edits before wasting a full test run! Persist progress notes to disk (Anthropic long-running harnesses) across compactions.
  4. Isolate execution from credentials. Run untrusted builds/tests inside unprivileged VMs with strict CPU/RAM timeouts, allowlisted package-mirror egress, and zero production/git-push credentials (the host harness pushes via a scoped proxy only after checks pass).
  5. Close the verification loop & protect tests from reward hacking. Run the reproduction test, impacted test targets, typecheckers, linters, and UI preview screenshots. Crucially, make existing test assertions read-only or run hidden acceptance checks in CI/eval—otherwise RL-trained or persistent agents learn they can turn a red suite green by deleting the failing test!
  6. Return an auditable PR. Output a minimal diff, root-cause write-up, and exact verification logs.

How interviewers poke at this design

“Two background agents fix bugs in the same repo simultaneously—how do you prevent clobbering?” Each task gets its own Copy-on-Write worktree/VM branched off main (never sharing a mutable working directory!). At PR merge time, rebase onto latest main and re-run fast impacted CI checks inside the merge queue before landing.

“3% of integration tests are flaky—how do you stop the agent from chasing ghosts?” Track rolling per-test flake rates on main: auto-retry known flaky failures once, run suspect failures against the unedited base commit to prove causality, and cap identical edit-test retry loops at 2–3 attempts.

Reference Design 05 · Cold-Start Supervision, Distillation & Selective Labels

Bootstrapping an ML System from Scarce Labels

A classic Staff design prompt goes: “Classify 10 million transactions, merchants, or policy violations at 15 ms latency—starting with 150 labeled rows and $150/hour domain experts.” Standard supervised training stalls on Day 1; your real job is matching model capacity to your data regime while building a bias-aware data flywheel.

1. Lock your human benchmark first, then match model capacity to N

Never dump all 150 expert labels into model.fit()—if you do, you have zero honest signal to tell whether rules or synthetic labels actually help! Spend your initial SME budget writing a crisp adjudication rubric (checking inter-annotator Cohen’s κ) and locking a held-out gold evaluation + calibration set split by time or entity. Every noisy training trick gets graded strictly against that human gold slice.

Next, show Staff judgment by evolving your model architecture along the label-volume crossover curve rather than jumping straight to heavy fine-tuning:

  • Regime 1 (~100–500 labels, Day 1): End-to-end fine-tuning a 300M encoder memorizes noise immediately. Freeze strong pretrained embeddings (CLIP or a dense text bi-encoder) + simple domain counters and fit an L2-regularized logistic probe or shallow LightGBM—shipping a working baseline in an afternoon (Ramp’s merchant taxonomy playbook).
  • Regime 2 (~2k–15k human + high-precision silver labels): Add LoRA / contrastive encoder fine-tuning so domain-specific phrases separate cleanly in embedding space.
  • Regime 3 (~50k–200k+ teacher/weak labels): Distill into a compact 10 ms production student (GBDT or small DeBERTa/tiny encoder) that captures 95%+ of a slow frontier LLM’s accuracy at ~1/50th the serving cost.

2. Scaling noisy supervision & distilling without amplifying errors

To reach Regime 3 without waiting a year for human labels, combine offline LLM-as-Teacher annotation (structured JSON rubrics, prompt caching, and dropping low-margin disagreements across multi-prompt votes) with (Snorkel’s generative label model weighting correlated regex/graph/SQL heuristics properly instead of naive majority voting).

Train the fast student with (KL on temperature-scaled teacher probabilities for silver rows + up-weighted exact cross-entropy on gold human train rows), avoiding hard 0/1 pseudo-label cutoffs (p > 0.95) that starve the student of informative borderline cases! Finish with Platt or isotonic calibration on your clean human calibration slice so serving thresholds reflect real-world prevalence.

3. Active learning & defeating selective-label bias in production

Once v1 ships, route weekly SME labeling budgets using . Even more importantly in enforcement systems—fraud blocks, spam takedowns, credit declines—account for , a core lesson from Stripe Radar and agent-assisted risk pipelines: if your model automatically blocks everything with score > 0.85, you never observe whether blocked users would have paid honestly or charged back! Training v2 only on approved traffic blinds it to blocked patterns. Keep a tiny, dollar-capped 0.5–1% randomized let-through control group (or stratified human audit of blocked items) weighted by inverse propensity so precision tracking and v2 retraining stay unbiased.

How interviewers poke at this design

“Abuse prevalence is 0.02% (1-in-5,000)—labeling 2,000 random rows for your gold test set finds zero positives!” Use stratified importance sampling: partition traffic into high weak-signal/embedding score, medium score, and uniform random background strata; sample and human-label inside all three; then weight each stratum’s confusion matrix by its exact share of total traffic (P(stratum)) to recover an unbiased estimate of real-traffic precision and recall.
